# Jazz It Out: The Axeman of New Orleans

**15 Minute History** · narration script · 1,644 words · 9:53 finished (with the intro, title card, logo breaks, two plugs and the end screen)

**Driving question (cold open, answered in the last chapter):** How did a killer get a whole city to throw him a party?
**Answer:** He probably didn't. The attacks and the fear were real; newspapers gave the fear a name; innocent people were blamed again and again; and a letter that was almost certainly a prank gave a frightened city something to do with its fear. The party was real. The invitation almost certainly wasn't.

## 0:00 | Cold Open: The Sheet Music

A piece of sheet music. New Orleans, 1919.

On the cover, a whole family is playing as loud as they can. Piano. Trombone. A kid on a drum. And the door is wide open behind them.

The title: The Mysterious Axman's Jazz. And underneath, in smaller letters: Don't Scare Me, Papa.

That's a song about a serial killer. And people bought it.

Because on one night in March 1919, *almost every dance hall in New Orleans was packed*. Bands played in living rooms all over the city, until well after midnight. The city wasn't celebrating. A killer had told them to.

So here's the question I want to answer today: *How did a killer get a whole city to throw him a party?*

It starts in a corner grocery, with a hole in the back door.

## 0:52 | The Back Door

May 23rd, 1918. Uptown New Orleans, the corner of Upperline and Magnolia.

Joseph Maggio runs a grocery on the ground floor. He and his wife Catherine live in the back. Like a lot of corner grocers in the city, they're Italian immigrants.

Sometime before dawn, someone chisels a panel out of their back door. Just big enough to crawl through. Inside, the intruder finds an axe that belongs to the Maggios.

Joseph's brothers live next door. They find the couple a few hours later. Catherine is already dead. Joseph dies minutes later.

Here's what bothers the police. Money and jewelry are sitting right there, in plain sight. *Nobody took them.* So this wasn't a robbery.

The razor used in the attack belongs to Joseph's brother Andrew, a barber. Andrew is arrested. Then released. He says he's innocent, and nobody can prove otherwise.

And a block away, someone has written a message in chalk on the sidewalk: "Mrs. Maggio will sit up tonight, just like Mrs. Toney."

Police guessed that meant the wife of Anthony Schiambra, another Italian grocer, attacked back in 1912. Newspapers started digging up old cases from 1911, too. Were they connected? Nobody could prove that either.

Hold onto that back door. You're going to see it again.

## 2:02 | A Spy, a Hatchet and a Deathbed

Five weeks later. June 27th. Another grocery, another back room.

Louis Besumer, a grocer, and Harriet Lowe, the woman living with him, are attacked with a hatchet in the night. A bakery driver finds them the next morning. Alive. Barely.

Then things get strange. It's 1918. America is at war with Germany, and the whole country is jumpy about spies. Police find letters in Besumer's home written in foreign languages. And Harriet Lowe tells them he's a German spy.

He isn't. They hold him for two days and let him go.

But in August, Lowe goes in for surgery on her injuries, and she doesn't recover. Before she dies, she names her attacker. Louis Besumer.

So the man who was attacked is now charged with murder. He spends nine months in jail. At his trial in May 1919, the jury takes about ten minutes to find him not guilty.

Ten minutes. That's barely enough time to find your coat.

Remember Besumer. *He won't be the last person blamed for this.*

## 2:58 | A City Sitting Up at Night

Then August. And things speed up.

August 5th. Anna Schneider, eight months pregnant, wakes up to a dark figure standing over her bed. She's beaten badly. She survives, and two days later, she has a healthy baby girl.

August 10th. Joseph Romano, an elderly man living with his two nieces, is struck in the head. The nieces see a man running away. Heavy-set. Dark suit. Slouch hat. Romano dies two days later.

In the yard, police find a bloody axe. And on the back door, a panel chiseled away.

Now the newspapers have a name for him. *The Axeman.*

And New Orleans panics. People report a prowler on every block. Men sit up all night with shotguns across their knees. A few call the police to report that they've found an axe in their backyard. Which, in 1918, is where a lot of people kept their axe.

That's mass hysteria. *Fear spreading faster than the facts.*

And then, nothing. That fall, the flu shuts down the city's schools, churches and theaters. In November, the war ends. And for seven months, the Axeman is quiet.

## 4:10 | Why Italian Grocers?

So why corner grocers? And why so many Italian families?

By 1918, New Orleans has one of the biggest Italian communities in the South, most of them from Sicily. A lot of them run small groceries on corners all over the city, with the family living in the back.

To a lot of newspapers, that pointed one way. The Mafia. Or the Black Hand: gangs that sent letters demanding money, and threatened to kill you if you didn't pay. That really did happen in New Orleans.

But there's a problem. The Black Hand wanted money. *The Axeman never took any.*

And there's a darker history behind how fast people reached for "Mafia." In 1891, after the city's police chief was murdered, a mob broke into the New Orleans jail and killed 11 Italian men. Some of them had just been found not guilty. It's often called the largest mass lynching in American history.

So when the Axeman hit Italian homes, plenty of New Orleanians saw the Mafia. A lot of Italian families saw something else. *They were the ones being hunted.* And nobody seemed to be protecting them.

Same evidence. Two very different stories.

## 5:12 | Gretna

March 10th, 1919. Gretna, just across the Mississippi River from New Orleans.

Charles and Rosie Cortimiglia are asleep with their little daughter, Mary. Someone comes in through the back door. Charles and Rosie are badly hurt. Mary is killed in her mother's arms.

A neighbor, the grocer Iorlando Jordano, hears the screams and runs across the street to help.

When Rosie wakes up in the hospital, she says the attackers were Jordano and his 18-year-old son, Frank.

Her husband says that isn't true. Iorlando was an old man, in poor health. Frank was more than six feet tall. Too big, many said, to fit through the hole in the door.

They're convicted anyway. Frank is sentenced to hang. His father gets life in prison.

In December 1920, Rosie Cortimiglia comes forward and admits she lied. The Jordanos are set free.

A child was dead. Two innocent men had nearly died for it. *And the Axeman was still out there.*

## 6:12 | A Letter from Hell

Three days after Gretna, a letter turns up at the newspapers. It's dated "Hell, March 13th, 1919." And the Times-Picayune prints it.

"Esteemed Mortal," it begins. "They have never caught me and they never will."

The writer says he isn't human. He's "a spirit and a demon from the hottest hell." He brags about how stupid the police are. And then he makes an offer.

He will pass over New Orleans at 12:15 on Tuesday night, St. Joseph's Night. "I am very fond of jazz music," he writes, "and I swear by all the devils in the nether regions that every person shall be spared in whose home a jazz band is in full swing."

Anyone who doesn't "jazz it out," he says, "will get the axe."

Now remember, in 1919, jazz is new. Plenty of respectable people think it's just noise.

And on Tuesday night, New Orleans jazzed it out. *The dance halls were packed.* Bands played house parties all over town. People who couldn't hire a band reportedly played records, or pounded on the family piano. And a local songwriter, Joseph Davilla, wrote a song about it. Yes. That's our sheet music.

Midnight. 12:15. Then morning.

*Nobody was attacked that night.*

So did the Axeman keep his word? Probably not, because he probably never wrote it. Even at the time, a rival paper, the New Orleans Herald, called it a "joke-letter." "Someone put one over on the Times-Picayune," it said. A prankster? A reporter? Maybe someone who'd make money off all that jazz. Nobody ever proved who wrote it.

The party was real. *The invitation almost certainly wasn't.*

## 7:43 | The Last Door

But whoever the Axeman was, he wasn't finished.

August 10th, 1919. A grocer named Steve Boca wakes up to a figure standing over his bed. He survives, with no memory of the attack. A panel has been chiseled out of his back door.

September 3rd. Sarah Laumann, 19 and living alone, is found unconscious in her bed. She survives too. A bloody axe lies on the lawn.

October 27th. Mike Pepitone, a grocer and a father of six. His wife hears a noise and sees a large man run from the room. Pepitone dies of his wounds.

That's the last attack anyone has tied to the Axeman.

He doesn't get caught. He doesn't write again. *He just stops.*

## 8:25 | So Who Was the Axeman?

So who was he?

A couple of years later, newspapers reported that in Los Angeles, Mike Pepitone's widow had shot and killed a man. She said he was the man who murdered her husband.

It's a perfect ending. It's also hard to check. The records that would prove the man she shot was the Axeman have never turned up.

There are other suspects. A man with a long criminal record. A killer who was really after the women in each house. The Mafia. Copycats. Or several different attackers, stitched into one monster by the newspapers. Some of the attacks don't match the others at all.

So let's go back to the question. *How did a killer get a whole city to throw him a party?*

He probably didn't. The attacks were real, and so was the fear. A city where men sat up all night with shotguns. Newspapers that gave the fear a name. Innocent people blamed, again and again.

And then a letter, almost certainly fake, that finally gave people something to do with all that fear. Something they were good at.

New Orleans never learned the Axeman's name. So it answered him the way New Orleans answers almost everything.

*It played.*

## Fact-check flags

1. **Maggio date and details (ch02).** Most accounts give the early hours of May 23, 1918 (some say May 22). Corner of
   Upperline and Magnolia: Wikipedia, citing *Times-Picayune* May 23–24, 1918. Catherine died at the scene; Joseph died
   minutes after his brothers Jake and Andrew found them. Accounts disagree on whether the axe came first or the razor;
   the script avoids the order and the gore. "An axe that belongs to the Maggios": the victims' own axe in most accounts.
2. **Money left behind (ch02).** "Money and valuables left in plain sight were not stolen" (Wikipedia, citing Gibson
   2006). Some accounts specify jewelry; the script says "money and jewelry", which several accounts repeat. Could be
   softened to "money and valuables."
3. **Andrew Maggio (ch02).** The razor came from his barber shop; he was arrested and released (*Times-Picayune*,
   May 27, 1918, "Andrew Maggio Released; Says He is Innocent").
4. **Chalk message (ch02).** "Mrs. Maggio will sit up tonight just like Mrs. Toney." Wording varies slightly between
   retellings. The Schiambra link (attacked 1912, Joanna killed) is the police theory of the time, and the script says
   "Police guessed". The 1911 cases are questioned by recent researchers; the script says newspapers "dug them up" and
   "nobody could prove" a connection.
5. **Besumer and Lowe (ch03).** Attacked June 27, 1918 (some say June 28). Found by bakery wagon driver John Zanca. The
   German-spy claim, two days in custody, Lowe's deathbed accusation in August 1918 after failed surgery, nine months
   in jail, acquittal May 1, 1919 after about ten minutes: Wikipedia citing Katz 2010, p. 56. Lowe's relationship to
   Besumer is described variously (wife, mistress); the script says "the woman living with him."
6. **Schneider and Romano (ch04).** August 5 and August 10, 1918. Schneider eight months pregnant, baby girl two days
   later. Romano's nieces Pauline and Mary Bruno saw a "dark-skinned, heavy-set man" in a dark suit and slouch hat. The
   script omits "dark-skinned" (an unreliable witness detail that fed prejudice) and keeps "heavy-set". Bloody axe in
   the yard, back-door panel chiseled: Wikipedia.
7. **"Axeman" name and the panic (ch04).** The *Times-Picayune* ran "Police Believe Ax-Man May Be Active in the City"
   on August 6, 1918. Reports of prowlers and axes found in backyards: Katz 2010, p. 56. "Men sit up all night with
   shotguns" is a common description of the panic; treat as illustrative.
8. **Flu and armistice (ch04).** New Orleans closed schools, churches and theaters in October 1918 for the influenza
   epidemic; the armistice was November 11, 1918. "For seven months, the Axeman is quiet": August 10, 1918 → March 10,
   1919.
9. **Italian community and the Black Hand (ch05).** New Orleans had the South's largest Italian community, mostly
   Sicilian. Black Hand extortion letters were real in New Orleans in the 1900s–1910s. The Axeman took nothing, which
   the script uses against the extortion theory.
10. **1891 lynching (ch05).** Police chief David Hennessy was shot in October 1890; on March 14, 1891 a mob stormed
    Parish Prison and killed 11 Italian men, some of whom had just been acquitted and others not yet tried. Usually
    described as the largest single mass lynching in U.S. history; the script hedges with "It's often called".
11. **Gretna (ch06).** March 10, 1919 (the Exoneration Registry says about 3 a.m., March 9–10). Mary killed in her
    mother's arms. **Ages differ between sources**, so the script gives none: Mary is "two years old" in the Exoneration
    Registry, "infant" on Wikipedia and "4-year-old" in the AP item of October 12, 1919; Iorlando is 68, 69 or (AP) 76;
    Frank 17 or 18. The script says "their little daughter, Mary" and "an old man, in poor health".
    Convicted; Frank sentenced to hang, Iorlando to life. Rosie recanted December 7, 1920 (Crime Library; National
    Registry of Exonerations). "Too big, many said, to fit through the hole in the door": Wikipedia/Katz.
12. **The letter (ch07).** Dated "Hell, March 13, 1919". Secondary sources give March 13, 14 or 16 for publication.
    *The Herald* (New Orleans), March 20, 1919 ("That Ax-Man's Letter", Chronicling America, shown on screen) says the
    letter "made good Sunday reading", so the *Times-Picayune* printed it on **Sunday, March 16, 1919**, matching HNOC
    and the St. Tammany library. The script says "Three days after Gretna, a letter turns up at the newspapers"
    (written March 13) "And the Times-Picayune prints it" (no date). Quoted lines are verbatim from
    the letter as reprinted: "Esteemed Mortal", "They have never caught me and they never will", "a spirit and a demon
    from the hottest hell" (some reprints: "a fell demon"), "I am very fond of jazz music, and I swear by all the devils
    in the nether regions that every person shall be spared in whose home a jazz band is in full swing", "jazz it out",
    "will get the axe".
13. **St. Joseph's Night (ch07).** March 19, 1919 was a Wednesday (St. Joseph's Day). The letter's "12:15 on next
    Tuesday night" is the night of March 18–19, St. Joseph's Night. Consistent.
14. **The party (ch07).** Dance halls full, bands at house parties: Katz 2010, p. 59; HNOC. "Played records or pounded
    on the family piano" is from later retellings and is hedged with "reportedly".
15. **The cover (ch01).** The script describes the real cover: a family playing piano, trombone and drum, the door
    open behind them. One line of the cover's small print, the title of Davilla's earlier song, contains a racial slur;
    the display copy used in the video covers that line (`img/ch01/mysterious_axman_jazz_cover_1919_display.jpg`). The
    original file is kept unaltered next to it.
15b. **The song (ch01, ch07).** *The Mysterious Axman's Jazz (Don't Scare Me Papa)*, Joseph John Davilla, arr. Jos.
    Garrow, World's Music Publishing Co., New Orleans, 1919 (HNOC 2008.0052). HNOC says Davilla wrote it "while he
    waited for the axman." **Cover description in ch01 (band, woman at the piano looking over her shoulder) must be
    checked against the scan** and adjusted if needed.
16. **"Probably never wrote it" (ch07).** No proof either way. Most modern accounts treat the letter as a hoax. The
    script now cites the contemporary evidence: *The Herald*, March 20, 1919, called it "this joke-letter" and said "it
    looks to us that someone put one over on the Times-Picayune" (quoted as "Someone put one over on the
    Times-Picayune," it said). The same editorial says the *Times-Picayune* "gloated over it Wednesday morning by
    publishing a cartoon showing one of the many families in a state of fright, the mother... playing jazz music":
    that cartoon is the sheet-music cover art ("Courtesy of The Times-Picayune, March 19, 1919").
17. **1919 attacks (ch08).** Steve Boca, August 10, 1919 (survived); Sarah Laumann, 19, September 3, 1919 (survived,
    attacked through an open window; the script doesn't mention a door for her); Mike Pepitone, October 27, 1919
    (killed; father of six). Last attack attributed. The AP item shown on screen (October 28, 1919) reported "blows
    from an iron bar" and "two assailants"; later accounts say one large man with an axe. The video notes the first
    reports in orange (a disputed claim) and the narration keeps "a large man".
18. **Los Angeles (ch09).** A 1921 newspaper story (dates given as December 1920 or December 1921) that Pepitone's
    widow, Esther Albano, shot a man called Joseph Mumfre/Manfre in Los Angeles and said he killed her husband.
    Researchers have not found independent records confirming it; the script says "A couple of years later,
    newspapers reported..." and "The records... have never turned up."
19. **Death toll (not stated).** At least six killed (HNOC): Joseph and Catherine Maggio, Harriet Lowe, Joseph Romano,
    Mary Cortimiglia, Mike Pepitone. The script never gives a total, to avoid the widely varying 6–17 estimates.
20. **"Axeman" vs "Axman".** Period papers and the sheet music used "Axman"; the narration uses "Axeman" (the common
    spelling today, and what viewers search). The sheet-music title is read as printed.
